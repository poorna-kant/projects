ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE. A generic planning distinction, described for the public demo.

**Unconstrained demand** is the quantity the business intends to fulfil, assuming supply is available. It is the output of the demand review and it is deliberately optimistic in the sense that it ignores feasibility.

**Constrained demand**, sometimes called supportable demand, is the quantity that available materials, capacity and lead times can actually support. It is the output of supply planning.

The distance between them is the planning gap, and it is the single most useful number in the cycle. It quantifies what the organisation must resolve: buy more, build more, re-prioritise, or tell someone they will not get what they asked for.

The common failure is not miscalculating either figure. It is quietly replacing one with the other. When an unconstrained plan is overwritten with what supply can support, the gap disappears from view, and the organisation stops being able to see the shortfall it is living with. The plan then looks achievable by construction, and the problem resurfaces only at execution, when it is expensive.

This is why Planned Demand in this demo is defined as unconstrained. It is not a prediction of what will ship. A separate measure answers the coverage question, and keeping the two apart is a deliberate design decision rather than an oversight.

The practical test when reading any demand figure: *has anything to do with supply been applied to this number?* If yes, it is not unconstrained demand, whatever it is labelled.
