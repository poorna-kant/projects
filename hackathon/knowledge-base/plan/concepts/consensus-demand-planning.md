ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE. This describes a generic, industry-standard planning practice for the public demo. It does not describe any particular organisation's process.

Consensus demand planning exists to solve an organisational problem rather than a mathematical one. Sales, marketing, finance and operations all hold a view of future demand, and each view is reasonable from where it is standing. If those views are never reconciled, every downstream function plans against a different number and the disagreement surfaces later as expediting, excess stock, or missed commitments.

The practice runs in a repeating cycle. A statistical baseline is generated from history. Participants review it and propose adjustments where they hold knowledge the history cannot contain: a promotion, a new customer, a discontinued line. Those adjustments are debated in a demand review and resolved into a single number. That number is then accepted as the plan of record.

Two properties matter more than accuracy.

The first is **singularity**. The value of a consensus plan is that there is only one. A more accurate number that only one function believes is worth less than a slightly worse number everyone plans against.

The second is **attribution**. Because the baseline and the adjustments are kept separately, it stays possible to ask whether the human judgement improved on the statistics or degraded it. If adjustment is absorbed into the plan without trace, that question becomes unanswerable, and the organisation loses its ability to learn from its own overrides.

Consensus demand is deliberately unconstrained. It states what the business intends to sell, not what it is able to supply. Introducing supply feasibility at this stage collapses two separate questions into one number and hides the gap between ambition and capability, which is precisely the gap the later planning stages exist to manage.
